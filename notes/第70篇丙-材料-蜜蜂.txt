The honeybee is the American insect: on the state seal of Utah, the state insect of a dozen more,
in every child's book of the farm, the maker of the honey in the cupboard and the wax in the
candle. Nothing seems more native to a meadow than a bee.

It came by ship. The Virginia Company's fleet that reached Jamestown in April 1622 carried hives
in the hold, two years after the Mayflower; by 1639 escaped swarms were nesting in the Massachusetts
woods. The settlers wanted honey, because sugar was rare, and beeswax, because tallow candles
stank, and mead. Pollination was not on the list; nobody valued that until the end of the
nineteenth century.

The bees went west faster than the wagons. Swarms leave a hive every spring and fly to a hollow
tree, and each new hollow was a day's flight further out; the Indians, Jefferson wrote, called the
honeybee the white man's fly, because where it appeared the settlers came a season behind. It still
took 231 years to reach the Pacific — Oregon and California in 1853, by sea again.

The continent was not empty of bees. It had bumblebees and hundreds of solitary bees, and the Maya
had kept stingless bees for a thousand years. It had even had honeybees once: a worker bee lies in
fourteen-million-year-old shale in Nevada. The genus had died out here long before it sailed back.

Today the white man's fly still moves ahead of the crop. Every February most of the country's hives
go to California on flatbed trucks, stacked on pallets, to pollinate the almonds, and then on to the
apples and the cherries, following the bloom like a herd. The bee is not native to the meadow. It is
native to the road.
