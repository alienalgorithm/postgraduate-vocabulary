A **surety** works because the man behind the debt stands outside it. His own **purse** is untouched by the **debtor**'s **default**. Reinsurance is that idea at scale: a **syndicate** which has **underwritten** more than it can bear **transfers** part of the **exposure** to a separate **pool** of **collateral**.

At Lloyd's in the 1980s the pools stopped being separate. Syndicate A **guaranteed** B's excess, B **guaranteed** C's, and C, in the end, **guaranteed** A's. The excess-of-loss **spiral** was a **circle**. One claim **travelled** round it, each of the **underwriters** keeping a cut of the **premium** and passing the balance on, until the same loss had been **redeemed** several times from one **pool**.

The **circle** was **accidental**, and its makers were **unwitting**. A court found later that even the men **dealing** in it could not say what the **spiral** did to a loss. Each **guaranteed** the next man's **liability**, **ignorant** of whose it was and **unaware** that the **pledge** ran home.

Then the claims arrived. **Mineral** **fibre** policies drafted in the 1930s matured in the 1980s, when **lung** cases reached the American **tort** courts. Under the year-of-account rules the member who **forfeited** his house was whoever held the **syndicate** now, not whoever signed the risk then. **Membership** **peaked** at 33,500 in 1988, the first of five losing years. Sixteen billion in **losses**; thousands **insolvent**.

A **guarantee** is worth something only while the guarantor stands outside the danger, his **collateral** beyond reach of what he backs. When every **syndicate** backs every other, a market has not spread its **exposure**. It has lost track of where the **exposure** sits. The **ruin** was not bad luck; the **litigation** that followed found only a **circle**, and inside it nobody had been outside anything.
