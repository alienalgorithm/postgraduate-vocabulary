The **factory-loaf** is remembered for speed: three and a half hours from flour to sliced and **wrapped**, against a night of **leaven**. That was not what the men at Chorleywood were after in 1961, and the thing they were after is the one nobody names.

Britain grows **soft** wheat. A **climate** of **drizzle** gives a grain low in **protein**, and low protein means weak **gluten**, and weak gluten will not hold a **loaf**. For a century the British **baker** had bought hard wheat from the Canadian prairie and mixed it in. The bread stood up because the wheat was imported.

Chamberlain, Collins and Elton found another way to make gluten. Instead of letting time and **yeast** do it overnight, a high-speed **mixer** put the energy in by force, in about three minutes; a **vitamin**, hard fat and extra yeast **strengthened** the rest. Weak flour came out with the **starch** and structure of strong. The bowl ran so hot it needed a cooling **jacket**.

The speed followed from that. By 2009 four **loaves** in five sold in Britain came from that **bakery** line: **kneaded** by machine, **baked** in **tins**, softer, keeping longer, cheaper, and free of the prairie. Since then the wheat itself has been bred up: flour of 11.5 to 13.5 per cent protein can now be grown at home.

The process is blamed for what it did to bread and praised for what it did to time. It was built for neither. It was built so that a country whose climate could not grow bread wheat could stop importing it. The thing remembered is the by-product; the thing forgotten is the constraint.
