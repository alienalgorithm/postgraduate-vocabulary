The bayonet charge is remembered as the decisive act of the musket age: the volley, the drums, the
line going in with cold steel, the enemy broken on the point. Recruits drilled the thrust and parry
for months; generals wrote their memoirs around it.

The surgeons kept another book. Larrey, Napoleon's surgeon-general, said that for every bayonet
wound he dressed there were a hundred from small arms or cannon. Eighteenth-century casualty
returns put bayonet wounds below 2 per cent. Corvisier's figure is 2.4. In the American Civil
War the Medical and Surgical History logged 922 sabre and bayonet cases across the whole war; in
three months of close fighting before Richmond the Union treated 32,000 bullet wounds and 37
bayonet thrusts. By 1914 the wounds were too few for their own column and went under
"miscellaneous."

Jomini, who served in those armies, explained why. Most bayonet charges in the open ended before
contact: one line or the other broke and ran. Steel was used at close quarters only where men
could not run — storming a breach, an ambush in broken ground. A bullet was random and unseen; a
bayonet was a personal, certain, gruesome end coming up the field at walking pace. Men left before
it arrived.

So the bayonet took ground it never touched. Between charges the blade was a candle-holder, a
tent-peg, a spit for the ration meat, a tool for digging in. Doctrine kept the charge into 1914
because it moved men forward under fire, not because it cut anyone.

The general's book and the surgeon's are two different ledgers. One records what took the field;
the other, what took the men. The weapon that decided the day is the one the hospital never saw.
