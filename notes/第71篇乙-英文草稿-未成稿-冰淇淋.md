**Ice-cream** is **cream** frozen with ice: **sugar**, **vanilla**, **egg-yolks**, a **custard** packed in a **bucket** of ice, a handle turned, a **scoop** at the end. The name says so, and so does the **picnic** photograph.

Ice alone cannot do it. Ice sits at the **freezing-point** of water, and a bowl of sweet cream freezes several degrees below that; bury the bowl in ice all afternoon and you get cold soup. The secret is **rock-salt**. Salt makes ice melt, and melting ice draws heat out of everything it touches; a tub of crushed ice and salt falls to about twenty degrees below zero. The cold that freezes the cream is the cold of ice melting.

The trick was written down in 1558 in **Naples**, in Giambattista della Porta's Natural Magic, as a way to freeze **wine** for a summer feast: snow in a wooden vessel, powdered saltpetre strewn on it, the flask turned in the snow until the wine congealed. Within a century cooks found that common salt did what saltpetre did, and a **pewter** **canister** of sweetened cream turned in a tub of salted ice became the **sorbet** maker, opened every few minutes to scrape the **crystals** off the wall.

What made it a household thing was a bucket with a **hand-crank**. In 1843 Nancy Johnson, a **housewife** of **Philadelphia**, patented a **freezer-tub**: an inner **churn**, a **dasher** turning inside it, an **insulated** tub of ice and salt round the outside, and a handle so the scraping never stopped. She sold the patent for two hundred dollars. By 1851 a Baltimore dairyman with too much cream had a factory; by the 1880s the **soda-fountain** had the **sundae** and the **wafer**.

The ice was never the cold. The cold was the salt undoing the ice, and the invention was a handle that kept the **paddle** scraping while the ice came apart.
