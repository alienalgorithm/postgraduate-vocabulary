The washing machine is the emblem of labour saved: the copper, the washboard, the mangle and the
wringer gone from the scullery, Monday no longer lost to suds and steam. Advertisements from the
1920s promised the housewife her afternoons back.

The time-budgets say otherwise. Studies of American women in 1924 found about 52 hours a week on
housework; studies in the 1960s found 55. A 2006 review put the figure between 51 and 56 in every
decade from the 1910s to the 1960s. A long-term study of the electric washer from 1925 to 1965 found
laundry taking more time at the end than at the start.

Three things absorbed the saving. Servants left: household servants per thousand people fell from
98.9 in 1900 to 58 by 1920, and immigration law cut the supply through the 1920s. The steam laundry
left: by the 1920s one stood in almost every urban neighbourhood, taking in the household wash, and
the home machine drove it out of business. And the standard rose: bedclothes washed monthly became
weekly; clothes worn for a week were changed daily. The machine did not shorten the task. It moved
the task into the house and raised the bar.

The laundress and the steam laundry had taken the work out of the home. The machine brought it
back, and put it on one woman. What was saved was the drudgery — the hauling, the boiling, the
wringing by hand. What was not saved was the hour.
