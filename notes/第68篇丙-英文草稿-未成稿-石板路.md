Picture a Dickens street and you hear it: **iron-shod** horses and iron-tyred wheels **clattering** over **cobbles**, the **cab** and the **omnibus** in a **rut** of old stone, the **kerb** worn round, medieval underfoot.

The stones were not old or cobbles. A **cobblestone** is a rounded beach stone; London's streets from the 1820s were **granite** **quarried** at **Aberdeen** and laid in **courses** across the road, an answer to one question: how does a horse keep its feet. Each block had an edge for a **hoof** to catch; after 1844 they were cut smaller for more edges. Hard stone **polished** till it was **slippery** as ice — Newgate Street's Welsh **paving** came up after two years — so engineers chose stone that wore rough.

The price of a foothold was noise. By the 1840s the Times ran letters weekly about the **din**; Dickens said the street outside Leech's study had killed him. So the city tried quiet: **macadam**, which wore into holes; **pine** **wood-block** outside the Old Bailey in 1839, **treacherous** wet, **rotten** in three years; **asphalt** in Threadneedle Street in 1869, smooth as **marble**, silent, **lethal** to a horse on a damp morning when it **skidded** and **stumbled**. **Horse-owners** petitioned; the RSPCA joined; the horses, one paper noted, could not vote.

Granite held the busy streets into the twentieth century; wood was still on a fifth of London's roads in 1929. The surface changed only when the hoof left it: the **tyre**, the **tram**, the **motor-car**, the **steamroller** and its **tarmac**, a road for **pedestrians** and **cartwheels** that no longer had to hold a horse.

Most of the granite is still there, **underneath**, **resurfaced** over; where it is **dug-up** it goes to **landscaping**, a **patio**, a **driveway**, sold as **heritage**. The cobbled street is not a relic. It is a compromise between the ear and the hoof, and the hoof won.
