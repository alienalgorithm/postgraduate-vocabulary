The dead hand is the usual figure for the English entail: a landowner reaching out of the grave to bind
his descendants. The law never allowed it. An entail could run for lives in being plus twenty-one years
and no further — a father, his eldest son, and that son's unborn son until his majority. Three
generations, then the chain lapsed.

So the great estates were not locked. They were relocked, once a generation, by agreement.

The bargaining position was symmetrical. The sitting holder was tenant for life: he drew the rents but
could not sell an acre. His heir held the reversion in tail: he owned the future but drew nothing until
his father died. Each had exactly what the other lacked.

On the heir's twenty-first birthday the two of them sat down. The son surrendered his tail, taking in
exchange an annuity payable at once. The father surrendered nothing he had, and gained another
generation of settlement. Portions for daughters, jointure for the widow, provision for younger sons —
all of it was negotiated in the same hour.

The perpetuity was never in the instrument. It was in a bargain struck fresh every twenty-one years
between two men who each held one thing the other wanted. A dead hand grips nothing. What held these
estates was a living hand, repeated.
