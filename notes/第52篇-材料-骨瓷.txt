The usual account has English potters unable to buy kaolin and reaching for bone ash as a makeshift.
Thomas Frye burned cattle bone at Bow in 1748 and got something white and translucent out of the kiln.

The obstacle then disappeared. William Cookworthy found china clay and china stone in Cornwall and
patented true hard-paste porcelain in 1768. The patent passed to Champion, then to a Staffordshire
consortium, and lapsed in 1796. From that year any potter in England could work the real material.

Almost none did. Josiah Spode had fixed his formula around 1790 and it became the English standard
instead: six parts bone ash, four of china stone, three and a half of china clay.

Read that recipe again. The china clay is in it. The china stone is in it. Bone ash was never standing
in for the missing ingredient, because the ingredient is not missing from the mixture. It is a third
thing added to both.

What bone ash bought was not availability. It was a body that fired whiter, chipped less, and stayed
translucent when thrown thinner than hard paste would bear. The makeshift outlived the shortage because
it had stopped being a makeshift some time before anyone noticed.
