Ice cream is cream frozen with ice: pack the tub in ice, turn the handle, wait. The name says so.

Ice alone cannot do it. Ice sits at the freezing point of water, and a bowl of sweet cream freezes
several degrees below that; you can bury the bowl in ice all afternoon and get cold soup. The
secret is salt. Salt makes ice melt, and melting ice draws heat out of everything it touches; a
bucket of crushed ice and rock salt falls to about twenty degrees below zero. The cold that freezes
the cream is the cold of ice melting.

The trick was written down in 1558 in Naples, in Giambattista della Porta's Natural Magic, as a way
to freeze wine for a summer feast: snow in a wooden vessel, powdered saltpetre strewn on it, the
flask turned in the snow until the wine congealed. Within a century cooks found that common salt did
what saltpetre did, and a pewter canister of sweetened cream turned in a tub of salted ice became
the sorbetiere, opened every few minutes to scrape the crystals off the wall.

What made it a household thing was a bucket with a crank. In 1843 Nancy Johnson of Philadelphia
patented a freezer: an inner cylinder, a dasher that turned inside it, an insulated tub of ice and
salt round the outside, and a handle so the scraping was continuous. She sold the patent for two
hundred dollars. By 1851 a Baltimore dairyman with too much cream had a factory; by the 1880s
the soda-fountain had the sundae.

The ice was never the cold. The cold was the salt undoing the ice, and the invention was a handle
that kept the scraping going while the ice came apart.
