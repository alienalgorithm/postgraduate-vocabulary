In February 1875 a boy named George Brewster was sent up the flues of Fulbourn asylum near
Cambridge, stuck, and smothered; a wall was pulled down to get him out. Lord Shaftesbury took the
inquest to the Lords, and the Chimney Sweepers Act of that year is remembered as the law that ended
the climbing boys.

It did not ban them. The ban was thirty-five years old. The Act of 1840 had made it an offence to
send anyone under twenty-one up or down a flue, and the Act of 1834 before it had fixed the shape
of new chimneys — fourteen inches by nine, no sharp angles — so that a machine could do the work. The
machine existed too: George Smart's brush of 1803, a bristle head on jointed canes pushed up from
the hearth, backed by a Society for Superseding the Necessity of Climbing Boys. For seventy years
the law, the tool and the lobby were all in place, and boys went on climbing.

They climbed because a brush cost money and a boy cost less; because old flues twisted where a
cane would not go; because a master sweep bought an apprentice from the parish for a few pounds;
and because nobody was paid to look. Shaftesbury told the Lords in 1875 that London, with four
million people, had no climbing boy left; the deaths were in the provinces, where no one enforced
what the metropolis had stopped doing.

The 1875 Act added two things: a sweep needed a licence from the police, renewed yearly, and a
constable could take it away. That was all. Within a decade the boys were gone.

The law that is remembered is not the one that made the thing illegal. It is the one that hired
someone to check.
