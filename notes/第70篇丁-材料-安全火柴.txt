The safety match is the one that will not light by accident: strike it on the box and it flares;
strike it on your boot, a wall or a thumbnail and nothing happens. Safe, the word on the box says,
and every kitchen drawer has one.

The head of a safety match is not safer than the head of the old lucifer. What the Swedes did in
1855 was take the phosphorus out of the match altogether and paint it on the side of the box. The
match head kept its chlorate; the striking surface got red phosphorus and ground glass; friction
between the two makes the flame. The safety is in the box. The match is the same splinter of poplar
dipped in the same fire.

The danger had never been the flame. It was the other phosphorus, the white kind, in the head of
the lucifer, the strike-anywhere match that lit on any rough surface. In the dipping rooms its
fumes rotted the jaw: toothache, then abscess, then the bone itself, which glowed in the dark and
came away in pieces. A Vienna doctor named the disease in 1839; Dickens wrote it up in 1852.

The safe version existed from 1855 and the factories did not want it. Bryant & May opened at Bow
in 1861 making Swedish red-phosphorus matches, then switched to white because customers liked a
match that lit on a wall. Of twenty-five British match works in 1888 twenty-three used white
phosphorus. The 1,400 women who struck that year won an end to the fines and kept the fumes; white
phosphorus was banned in Britain in 1910, fifty-five years after the box that made it unnecessary.

The word safety was printed on the box because that was where the safety was. The match was never
the problem. The box was the cure, and it took half a century for anyone to want it.
