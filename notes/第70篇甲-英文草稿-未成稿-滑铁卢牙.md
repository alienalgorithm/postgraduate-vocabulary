Waterloo teeth: the **dentures** of Regency England, the story goes, held the **incisors** and **canines** of young men killed in June 1815, pulled with **pliers** from the **battlefield** by **scavengers** who followed the armies, sorted into matched sets and **riveted** to a base of **hippopotamus** or **walrus** **ivory** for the mouths of the rich.

The name was a brand. Ivory teeth looked dead and rotted; a human tooth on an ivory plate was the best a **toothless** gentleman could buy. The supply, for a century before Waterloo and half a century after, was the **graveyard**. The **grave-robber** who sold **corpses** to the anatomy schools kept the teeth as a perk, and often did not trouble with the **corpse** at all, since the teeth alone paid for the night's **shovel** work. Other teeth came from the **hangman**, the **dissecting-room** and the pauper's ward.

So Waterloo was not a provenance but a promise: these came from a healthy man of twenty killed by a cannonball, not from a hanged thief or a consumptive dug up in Lambeth. **Dentists** advertised Waterloo ivory for decades after the battle. The set in the Science Museum labelled Waterloo teeth was pulled by **looters** on the battlefields of the American Civil War, half a century later, and shipped to Europe by the barrel.

What ended the trade was not disgust. It was **porcelain** that no longer chipped; gold plates and springs that let a man talk and go on **chewing**; a hard rubber base cheaper than ivory; and the Anatomy Act of 1832, which gave the schools a legal supply of bodies and took the grave-robber's night work away. The teeth of the dead went out when something cheaper came in.

A brand does not say where a thing came from. It says where the buyer would like to think it came from.
