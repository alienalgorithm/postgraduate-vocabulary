Every **bottle** says it: brewed to the Reinheitsgebot of 1516, the purity law, the oldest food rule in the world — barley, **hops** and water, no **preservatives**, five centuries of **froth** on **pilsner**, **lager** and **ale**, **tapped** the same in every **beer-garden**.

The decree of 23 April 1516, signed at Ingolstadt by the **dukes** of **Bavaria**, is mostly a price list. A Mass was **capped** at one **Munich** Pfennig in winter, two in summer; the **innkeeper**'s margin was fixed; bad beer faced **confiscation** by the **excise-man**; in a **scarcity** of barley the dukes could **curtail** brewing. Then the sentence everyone remembers: barley, hops and water, nothing else. The word purity is not in it. Neither is yeast.

The sentence was about bread. Wheat and **rye** made **loaves**; barley made poor bread and good **malt**, so brewers were kept off the **bakers**' **grist** in a duchy where a bad harvest meant riot. It also **banned** the northern brewers' **herbs** and the cheaper tricks — **soot**, **nettle**, **mushrooms** — that stretched a cask. It did not bind the men who signed it: the **nobility** kept **wheat-beer** for their own table.

The name came four hundred years later: a Bavarian **parliament** man coined Reinheitsgebot in 1918. In 1987 the European Court in **Luxembourg** **repealed** it as **protectionism**, on a **complaint** to **Brussels** from French brewers whose beer, made with **additives** beside **malted** grain, could not be sold as Bier. The labels kept the word.

A grain ration and a price cap became a purity, then a **heritage**, then a **badge** on the **stein** and a **slogan** on the **mug**; in 2016 the **anniversary** was a **jubilee**, with **marketing** to match. The rule did not change in five hundred years. What it was for changed three times.
