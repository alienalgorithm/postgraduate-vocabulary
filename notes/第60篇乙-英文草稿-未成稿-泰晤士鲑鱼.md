In 1974 a salmon was found on the **tidal** Thames, the first in 140 years, and the river was declared cured. **Sewage** works and **oxygen** **barges** had done it; the **effluent** was gone, the **dredge** had cleared the bed, the **sluice** ran clear, and the fish had come home.

It had not come home. It was one **stray** female, stuck on a power-station **grille**, from nobody knew which river. From 1975 the Thames was stocked from a **hatchery** on a Scottish **loch**: 60,000 to 80,000 **parr** and **fry** a year, released into each **brook** and **tributary** above the **weir**. About one in a hundred came back.

Through the 1980s the run rose, to over 300 in the early 1990s, and every fish carried a **tag**. They were **homing** to water they had been poured into, not to a **redd** they had been born in; no wild **roe** was ever found. After 1995 the run fell, and in 2005 the **seine** and the **trawl** recorded none.

Then the geneticists checked. Against 3,830 fish from across southern Europe they placed 16 untagged salmon caught between 2005 and 2008. Not one matched the released stock. Every one was a **migrant** from another river in the south of England — a **stray**, like the first, and a **wanderer** by **gill** and nature.

Thirty years of **restocking** under a **rehabilitation** scheme and a **charity** had founded nothing. The **hatchery** was a **prop**, the annual **restock** a **subsidy**, and when the **crutch** was withdrawn the **headcount** went to zero. What swims up the Thames now came on its own. The **imprint** was never on the fish. You can count what returns; you cannot count, from that, whether the river made it.
