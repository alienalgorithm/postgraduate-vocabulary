The safety match is the one that will not light by accident: strike it on the **matchbox** and it **flares**; strike it on your **boot**, a wall or a **thumbnail** and nothing happens. Safe, says the box, and every drawer has one.

The head is no safer than the old **lucifer**'s. What the **Swedish** makers did in 1855 was take the **phosphorus** out of the match and paint it on the box: the head kept its salts, the **striker** strip got red phosphorus and **emery**, and **friction** between the two makes the flame. The safety is in the box. The **matches** are the same **splints** of **poplar** **dipped** in the same **tipping**.

The danger had never been the flame. It was the other phosphorus, the white kind, in the head of the strike-anywhere match. In the **dipping** rooms its **fumes** rotted the **jaw**: toothache, then an **abscess**, then the bone itself, which glowed in the dark and came away in pieces. A Vienna doctor named the disease in 1839; Dickens wrote it up in 1852.

The safe version existed and the **factory** did not want it. Bryant & May, **Quaker** makers at Bow, began with the Swedish match and switched to white, because a customer liked a match that lit on a wall and a wall cost nothing. No **ventilation** was fitted; **fines** were docked for talking; twenty-three of twenty-five British works used the white head. The 1,400 women who struck in 1888 won an end to the fines and kept the fumes.

White phosphorus was banned in Britain in 1910, fifty-five years after the box that made it unnecessary. The word safety was printed on the box because that was where the safety was. The match was never the problem; the box was the cure, and it took half a century for anyone to want it.
