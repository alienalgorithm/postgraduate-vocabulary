The story is that George Hodgson invented a beer strong enough to reach India. Nothing needed inventing.
Ale and porter had gone out to Calcutta since the early 1700s and arrived sound; a cask of porter could
be excellent after a year at sea.

Read the auction notices. From the 1780s the Calcutta papers advertised captains' private cargoes: pale
ale, porter, cider — and small beer, the weakest thing a brewhouse makes. If small beer survived the
passage, the passage did not demand strength.

What Hodgson shipped already existed. October beer was brewed at harvest for country estates and cellared
two years, heavily hopped, meant to answer the like purpose of wine. A manual of 1768 gives the recipe
with six and a half pounds of hops to eight bushels of malt, to ensure a year's keeping. That was written
before Hodgson sent a single cask east.

His advantage was a wharf. The Bow brewery stood beside the East India docks, and he offered captains
eighteen months' credit.

The voyage was not the obstacle. Four months of pitching and rising heat matured the cask faster than any
cellar could: the beer that landed was not the beer that sailed. The trade did not find a brew that could
survive the journey. The journey was the last stage of the brewing.
