The **bayonet** charge is the set piece of the **musket** age: the **volley**, the **bugle**, the line going in behind eighteen inches of **blade**. Recruits spent months in **barracks** at the **parry** and the **lunge**; generals built **memoirs** around the moment the **sentry** saw the line come on.

The surgeons kept another book. Larrey, Napoleon's surgeon-general, **dressed** a hundred bullet or **cannon** wounds with **lint** for every one from a bayonet. Before Richmond in 1862 the Union **infirmary** took 32,000 bullet wounds to its **gauze** and **splint**, and 37 from the bayonet; by 1914 such **casualties** were too few for their own column.

Jomini explained why. Most charges in the open ended before contact: one line **fled**, and the **rout** came before any **impaling**. Steel met flesh only where men could not run — a **breach** stormed, an **ambush** in broken ground. A **canister** ball was random and unseen; a bayonet was a certain end at walking pace, a **spear** with a man behind it.

So the bayonet took ground it never touched. Between charges the **socket** blade was a candle-holder, a tent-peg, a thing to **dig** with; the **quartermaster** issued it, the **cartridge** did the killing. Doctrine kept the charge into 1914 because it made men **advance** under fire and **wield** something the enemy could see.

The **pike** was gone by 1700; the bayonet came out of its **scabbard** for the night **raid** and hangs in every **armoury** since, kept for **morale**, not the wound. The general's book and the **matron**'s **subtotal** are two accounts of one afternoon. The weapon that decided the day was the one the infirmary never saw.
