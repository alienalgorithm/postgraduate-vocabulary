Scurvy took eighty of the three hundred and fifty men aboard the Salisbury within ten weeks. On 20 May
1747 the surgeon James Lind picked twelve of the sick, split them into six pairs, and gave each pair a
different remedy: cider, vitriol, vinegar, seawater, a paste of garlic and mustard, and two oranges with
a lemon. Only the citrus pair mended, one fit for duty inside six days.

Lind printed his treatise in 1753. The Admiralty did nothing for forty-two years, calling lemons too
costly. Gilbert Blane finally won the order in 1795: three quarters of an ounce of lemon juice to every
sailor daily. Scurvy left the fleet.

Then it returned. The Navy swapped Mediterranean lemons for limes grown in its own Caribbean colonies,
cheaper and imperial. A lime holds a fraction of the antiscorbutic strength of a lemon, and the juice was
often boiled and stored in copper, which wrecks what survives. Polar parties of the eighteen-seventies
carried lime juice and still lost teeth.

The nickname limey dates from the swap. It commemorates the moment the remedy was weakened, not the
moment it worked.

Nobody could tell lemon from lime in the way that mattered, because nobody knew what the active
substance was. Vitamin C was not isolated until 1932. A remedy without a mechanism can be copied but
cannot be maintained: any substitution looks equivalent when the thing that matters is invisible.
