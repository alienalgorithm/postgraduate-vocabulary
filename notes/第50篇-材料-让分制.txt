A handicap in racing is weight added to the better horse. The handicapper's stated aim is a dead heat:
every runner crossing together. If he succeeds the race tells you nothing about which horse is faster,
which is the thing spectators came to learn.

The system was formalised at Newmarket in the eighteenth century and spread to golf, sailing and chess.
In each the same tension appears. A perfect handicap destroys the information the contest exists to
produce; an imperfect one lets the strong win as before.

What handicapping actually sells is closeness, not fairness. A field that finishes in a bunch produces
more betting turnover than one strung out over a furlong. The rating is adjusted after every race, so a
horse that wins is punished and a horse that loses is rewarded, and the ratings drift toward whatever
keeps the fields level rather than whatever measures the animals.
