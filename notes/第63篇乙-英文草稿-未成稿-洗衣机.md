The washing machine is the emblem of labour saved. The **advertisements** of the 1920s showed the **housewife** in gloves beside the new **appliance** and promised her afternoons back: the **drudgery** of Monday over, the **chores** of the week — **broom**, **mop**, **scouring** — done by noon.

Monday had been this: water carried and the **copper** boiled, **lye** **soap** shaved into it, each sheet **rinsed** in the **tub**, scrubbed on the **washboard**, **wrung** through the **mangle**, **starch** for the collars, **bleach** for the whites, the lot on the **clothesline** by noon.

The time-budgets say the afternoons never came. Women in 1924 logged about 52 hours a week of **housework**; women in the 1960s logged 55. A study that followed the electric washer from 1925 to 1965 found the **laundry** — **shirts**, **cuffs**, **blouse**, **apron** — taking longer at the end than the start, and the **hamper** never empty.

Three things absorbed the saving. The **maid** left, and the **immigration** acts of the 1920s cut the supply behind her. The **steam** laundry left: one had stood in almost every urban district, its **van** collecting the wash against a **ticket**, and the home machine drove it under. And the bar rose: sheets washed monthly became weekly, a shirt worn a week was worn a day, a **wardrobe** worn once was a wardrobe washed.

The machine's heirs did the same. The **dryer** ended the clothesline, **detergent** ended the copper, **nylon** and **drip-dry** ended the starch — and the **tumble** filled as fast as it emptied. The **housemaid** and the steam laundry had taken the work out of the house; the machine set it on one woman. What was saved was the wringing. What was not saved was the hour.
