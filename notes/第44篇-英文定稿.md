Scurvy took **eighty** of the Salisbury's 350 men in ten weeks. On 20 May 1747 James Lind split twelve of the sick into six pairs, each on a different remedy: **cider**, vitriol, **vinegar**, **seawater**, a paste of **garlic** and **mustard**, two **oranges** and a **lemon**. Only the **citrus** pair **mended**.

Lind printed his **treatise** in 1753. The Admiralty did nothing for 42 years: **lemons** were too **dear**. Gilbert Blane won the order in 1795: three quarters of an **ounce** of lemon **juice** per **sailor** a day. **Gums** stopped **bleeding**, **bruises** began to **heal**, and scurvy left the fleet.

Then it came back. From the 1860s the Navy **swapped** Mediterranean lemons for **limes** from its own Caribbean **colonies** — cheaper, and **imperial**. A **lime** holds a fraction of a lemon's **ascorbic** **potency**. The juice was **diluted**, boiled and kept in copper casks, which **wrecks** what survives. Polar parties of the **eighteen-seventies** carried it and **rotted**.

The nickname limey dates from that **exchange**. American sailors **dubbed** the British by that ration in the 1850s, and it **stuck**, **outliving** the fleet that earned it. But it **commemorates** the day the remedy was **blunted**, not the day it worked. The word the **Navy** is known by **celebrates** its **worst** call about that illness.

Nobody could tell lemon from lime in the way that mattered, because no **reasoning**, and no **recipe**, told them what to weigh. The **active** thing in them went **unnamed** until 1932. A **cure** without a **mechanism** can be **copied** but not kept: two things look **interchangeable** when what **divides** them is unseen. Lemon and lime were **indistinguishable** by every trial aboard.
