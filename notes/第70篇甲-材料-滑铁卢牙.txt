Waterloo teeth: the dentures of Regency England, the story goes, were filled with the incisors of
young men killed in June 1815, pulled with pliers from the field by scavengers who followed the
armies, boiled, sorted into matched sets and riveted to bases of hippopotamus ivory for the mouths
of the wealthy.

The name was a brand. False teeth carved from hippo, walrus or elephant ivory looked dead and
rotted; a human tooth on an ivory plate was the best a toothless gentleman could buy. The supply,
for a century before Waterloo and half a century after, was the grave. Resurrectionists who sold
corpses to the anatomy schools kept the teeth as a perk; often they did not trouble to take the
body at all, since the teeth alone paid for the night's work. Other teeth came from the gallows,
the dissecting-room and the pauper's ward.

So "Waterloo" was not a provenance but a promise: these came from a healthy man of twenty killed
by a cannonball, not from a hanged thief or a consumptive dug up in Lambeth. Dentists advertised
Waterloo ivory for decades after the battle. The set in the Science Museum labelled Waterloo teeth
was pulled on the battlefields of the American Civil War, half a century later, and shipped to
Europe by the barrel.

What ended the trade was not disgust. Porcelain teeth that no longer chipped, Claudius Ash's gold
plates and springs in the 1830s, Goodyear's vulcanite rubber base in the 1840s, and the Anatomy Act
of 1832, which gave the schools a legal supply of bodies and took the resurrectionist's night work
away. The teeth of the dead went out when something cheaper came in.

A brand does not describe where a thing came from. It describes where the buyer would like to
think it came from.
