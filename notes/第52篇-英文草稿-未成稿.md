The board ran nine spaces to a side: four corners, a **gaol**, a park, a row of **railway** **tiles**. Each was a **lease** or a **rent**. Every **throw** of the **dice** moved a **pawn** onto one. Lizzie Magie, a **stenographer** at the Dead Letter Office, patented it in 1904 and wrote two sets of rules for it.

Under **Prosperity**, rent went to a common **pot**. Everyone grew richer when any one of them took a **freehold** well. Under the other set you **bankrupted** your **opponent** and took his **deeds** and **tenancy**. Same board, same throws. The pair was the **lesson**: play both, feel the difference, judge for yourself.

Almost nobody played it. The **gentle** set was printed, sold, and left in the box. The **harsh** one travelled alone for thirty years, **handmade**, its **cash** cut from paper. Boards **inked** on cloth reached the **salon** and the **campus**, then a Quaker **parlour**, then an unemployed **salesman** in Germantown.

Parker Brothers took his board. Then Magie's patent: five hundred **dollars**, no **royalties**, **ownership** of two other designs thrown in. She had asked for none. He **retired** **wealthy** at forty-six on the **fortune** it made. In the count of 1940 she gave her **occupation** as maker of games and her **income** as a **zero**.

The company is the usual **villain**, and it earned the name. But the **choosing** was done three decades before it arrived. A **teaching** device with two halves keeps the **winnable** half. Players **chose** the **enjoyable** rules over the instructive ones, every time. Magie put the argument in the board and left it to the **tenant** and the **landlord** at her table. The **players** threw away the half that carried it.
