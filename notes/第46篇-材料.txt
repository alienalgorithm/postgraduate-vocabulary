On 20 June 1968 at Sacramento three sprinters were hand-timed at 9.9 seconds and all three were ratified
as world records. The electronic readings for the same races were 10.03, 10.10 and 10.14. The watches
had recorded a tie among men who finished eleven hundredths apart.

Hand timing was not sloppy. Three officials stood at the line with stopwatches and the middle value was
taken, a median chosen precisely to discard the outlier. What the method could not discard was a bias
shared by all three.

A timekeeper starts on the smoke of the pistol, which he must react to, and stops on a runner he has
been watching approach, which he can anticipate. The error is late at the start and early at the finish,
so the two do not cancel. They add. Every hand time is short by roughly a quarter of a second, and the
governing body eventually fixed the conversion at 0.24.

Fully automatic timing became compulsory on 1 January 1977. The old marks were not deleted but
reclassified, and a barrier that had been broken in 1968 was found still standing.

The instrument had been averaging away the thing it existed to detect. A median protects against a
timekeeper who is wrong. It does nothing against three who are wrong the same way.
