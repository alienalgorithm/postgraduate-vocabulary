For 157 years Cambridge published its mathematical results in order of merit, and the man at the top was called Senior Wrangler. Until 1850 the paper was **obligatory** even for men reading **classics**. Maxwell, Kelvin and Cayley all sat it as young men. That **placing** was held to be the highest **intellect**ual **prize** in the country, and years were remembered by whoever had taken it.

Hardy sat the paper in 1898 and wrote afterwards that when the examination stood at the **apex** of its difficulty and **renown**, English mathematical **prowess** was near its lowest **ebb**. The **prestige** of the paper and the health of the subject had moved in **opposite** directions for fifty years.

The training was not done by the university. Men hired private **instructors** who **crammed** them on old papers and on bookwork — the **verbatim** **recital** of proofs under timed conditions. **Attendance** at **lectures** fell away; the **famed** teachers were not the professors but men the professors never met. Babbage held the Lucasian chair from 1828 to 1839 and gave none in eleven years.

The **syllabus** **hardened** around whatever could be scored fast, and the **grading** shaped the teaching. Continental **analysis** was **neglected** because it was hard to set as a problem with one scorable answer. The best **mentors** could pick the **sharpest** men, which kept their **repute** up, which let them pick again.

The order of merit was **abolished** in 1909, and the private **tutors** were gone within a decade. Hardy went further: the only harmless examination, he argued, is one with a low pass mark and no classes at all. A **pecking** order does not measure the subject. It **becomes** the subject, and whatever goes ungraded **disappears** from the **curriculum** first.
