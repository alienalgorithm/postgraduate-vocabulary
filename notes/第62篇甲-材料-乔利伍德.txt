The factory loaf is remembered for speed: three and a half hours from flour to sliced and wrapped,
against a night of fermentation. That was not what the men at Chorleywood were after in 1961.

Britain grows soft wheat. A wet, cool summer gives a grain low in protein, and low protein means
weak gluten, and weak gluten will not hold a loaf. For a century British bakers had bought hard
wheat from the Canadian prairie and blended it in. The bread stood up because the wheat was
imported.

Chamberlain, Collins and Elton at the British Baking Industries Research Association found another
way to make gluten. Instead of letting time and yeast do it overnight, a high-speed mixer put the
energy in mechanically, in about three minutes; a little ascorbic acid, hard fat and extra yeast
did the rest. Weak British flour came out with the structure of strong Canadian flour. The mixing
ran so hot the bowl needed a cooling jacket.

The speed followed from that. By 2009 four loaves in five sold in Britain were made this way. The
loaf was softer, more uniform, kept longer, and cheaper, and the bakers no longer needed the prairie.
Since then British wheat itself has been bred up: flour of 11.5 to 13.5 per cent protein can now
be grown at home.

The process is blamed for what it did to bread and praised for what it did to time. It was built
to do neither. It was built so that a country whose climate could not grow bread wheat could stop
importing it. The thing remembered is the by-product; the thing forgotten is the constraint.
