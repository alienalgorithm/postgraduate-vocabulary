Everyone knows what the **spice** trade was for. Without a cold **larder** the meat went off, so the rich drowned it in **pepper**, **ginger** and **cinnamon** and ate it anyway. The **caravan** routes, the Portuguese voyages, the **bale** unloaded on the quay, the whole apparatus of empire — all of it to cover a smell.

Look at the cost. In the early fourteenth century a pig sold for the same money as a pound of **pepper**, the cheapest thing on the list. **Nutmeg**, **mace**, **clove** and **saffron** ran far dearer. A man who could not afford fresh pork could not afford a **pinch** of it to hide the taste of stale pork.

So the people rich enough to buy from the **bale** were exactly the people least likely to eat anything **tainted**. Their **pantry** held fresh meat because their **butcher** killed to order, the **shambles** working every morning. Everyone else preserved by **salting**, **pickling** and drying, which worked, and which cost almost nothing.

The story traces to one **chapter**: Drummond's *The Englishman's Food*, 1939. He took the medieval word *greene* to mean meat gone green, when it meant meat not yet hung long enough — an **errata** slip would have settled it. In the same **chapter** he printed the town **bylaws** that made a **butcher** **forfeit** **rancid** goods, the **guild** that enforced them, and the **whipping** that followed.

They were bought for **status**. A **platter** at a **banquet** was a **display**, and the **guest** was meant to count the cost. That is a poor sort of explanation and the mind refuses it, so a practical reason gets invented and outlives every correction. A story that gives a useful purpose to a **luxury** beats the true answer, which is that somebody wanted it.
