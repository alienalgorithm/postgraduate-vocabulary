Everyone knows what the **spice** trade was for. Without a cold **larder** the meat went off, so the rich drowned it in **pepper**, **ginger**, **clove** and **cinnamon**. The **caravan** routes, the Portuguese voyages, the **galley** at anchor, the **bale** swung onto the **quay** — all to cover a smell.

Look at the cost. In the early fourteenth century a pig sold for what a pound of pepper did, the cheapest item on the **consignment**. **Nutmeg**, **mace** and **saffron** ran dearer, before the **levy** at the quay. A man who could not buy fresh pork could not buy a **pinch** of **peppercorn** to hide stale pork.

So those rich enough to buy were those least likely to eat anything **tainted**. Their **pantry** held fresh meat because the **butcher** killed to order, the **shambles** working each morning under the **guild**. Everyone else used **salting**, **pickling** and drying: cheap methods that worked, leaving the spice and its caravan to the rich.

The story traces to one page of Drummond's *The Englishman's Food*, 1939. He took *greene* for meat gone green, when it meant meat not yet hung long enough — a **rancid** reading of a **butchery** term, printed and reprinted since. In the same **chapter** he printed the **bylaws** making a butcher **forfeit** bad goods, the **whipping** that followed, the **errata** his own **dictum** needed.

They were bought for **status**. A **platter** at a **banquet** was a **display** — the **goblet**, the **napkin**, the loaded **sideboard**, a **guest** meant to count the cost and **envy** it. That is a poor explanation, so a practical reason gets invented and outlives correction. A story giving a **luxury** some use beats the truth: somebody wanted it.
