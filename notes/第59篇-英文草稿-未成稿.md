Everyone knows what the **spice** trade was for. Without a cold **larder** the meat went off, so the rich drowned it in **pepper**, **ginger**, **clove** and **cinnamon**. The **caravan** routes, the Portuguese voyages, the **galley** at anchor, the **bale** on the **quay** — all to cover a smell.

Look at the cost. In the early fourteenth century a pig sold for what a pound of pepper did, the cheapest item on any **consignment**, **crate** or **cask**. **Nutmeg**, **mace** and **saffron** ran dearer, before the **levy** on the **lading**. A man who could not buy fresh pork could not buy a **pinch** of **peppercorn** to hide it.

So those rich enough to buy were those least likely to eat anything **tainted**. Their **pantry** held fresh meat: the **butcher** killed to order, the **shambles** worked each morning under the **guild**, the **spit** turned with its **basting** by noon. Everyone else used **salting**, **pickling** and drying in the yard: cheap, and they worked.

The story traces to one page of Drummond's *The Englishman's Food*, 1939. He took *greene* for meat gone green, when it meant meat not yet hung — a **rancid** reading of a **butchery** term. It travelled by **reprint** and **preface** because no **reviewer** checked it. The same **chapter** prints the **bylaws** that made a butcher **forfeit** bad goods and the **whipping** that followed — an **errata** he never saw.

They were bought for **status**. A **platter** at a **banquet** was a **display**: the **goblet**, the **napkin**, the loaded **sideboard**, a **guest** meant to count the cost and **envy** it. That is a poor explanation, so a practical reason gets invented and outlives correction. A story giving a **luxury** some use beats the truth: somebody wanted it.
