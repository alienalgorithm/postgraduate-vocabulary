Everyone knows what the **spice** trade was for. Without a cold **larder** the meat went off, so the rich drowned it in **pepper**, **ginger** and **cinnamon**. The **caravan** routes over Asia, the Portuguese voyages round Africa, the **galley** at anchor and the **bale** swung onto the **quay** — all of it to cover a smell.

Look at the cost. In the early fourteenth century a pig sold for what a pound of **pepper** sold for, and **pepper** was the cheapest item on the **consignment**. **Nutmeg**, **mace**, **clove** and **saffron** ran dearer, before the **levy** at the **warehouse** door. A man who could not buy fresh pork could not buy a **pinch** to hide stale pork.

So those rich enough to buy were exactly those least likely to eat anything **tainted**. Their **pantry** held fresh meat because the **butcher** killed to order, the **shambles** working each morning under the **guild**. Everyone else used **salting**, **pickling** and drying: cheap methods that worked, and that left the **spice** untouched in its **bale** on the **warehouse** floor.

The story traces to one page of Drummond's *The Englishman's Food*, 1939. He took *greene* to mean meat gone green, when it meant meat not yet hung long enough — a **rancid** reading of a word about **butchery**. In the same **chapter** he printed the **bylaws** that made a **butcher** **forfeit** **rancid** goods, the **whipping** that followed, and the **errata** his own **dictum** needed.

They were bought for **status**. A **platter** at a **banquet** was a **display**: the **goblet**, the **napkin**, the loaded **sideboard**, a **guest** meant to count the cost and **envy** it. That is a poor explanation, so a practical reason gets invented and outlives correction. A story giving a **luxury** some use beats the true answer: somebody wanted it.
