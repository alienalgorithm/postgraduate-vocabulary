Dorothy Sayers put it down to national **temper**: the Belgian plays a **tune** on his bells, the Englishman works out **permutations** in the **nave**. **Temperament** had nothing to do with it; the **wheel** did. A **melody** was never on offer from an English **tower** or **steeple**.

An English tower bell hangs from a **headstock** on a full wheel. It starts mouth **upward**, **swings** a whole **circle**, and rests mouth up on the far side, striking the **lip** at **handstroke** and back. The lightest in a **peal**, the **treble**, may be 600 pounds; the **tenor** runs to a ton, its **tuning** set at the **foundry**.

A ringer holds the **sallie** and can **hurry** that swing a little, or check it against the **slider**. He cannot stop it, nor play it quickly, nor **muffle** it mid-**swing**. So no bell shifts more than one place at each change, the **clapper** falling at **backstroke** as at hand — not a rule of taste but the limit of what a **rope** does to a turning ton of **metal**.

The Low Countries hung their bells dead in the **belfry**, bolted at the **crown** below the **spire**, struck by **hammers** from a **keyboard** of wooden **batons**. A **carillon** plays any tune written for it, and its player may sit above the **vestry** all afternoon.

Given a **rig** that could shuffle its notes one place at a time, the English wrote the only music such **rigging** makes. On ten bells the full set takes four months of unbroken work by a **rota** of **sexton** and **bellman**; only a **muffled** ring spares the **churchyard**. What looks like a national **temperament** is often the shape of the **headstock**.
