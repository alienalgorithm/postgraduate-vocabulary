Dorothy Sayers put it to national **temper**: the Belgian plays a **tune** on his bells, the Englishman works **permutations** from a **pew** in the **nave**. **Temperament** had nothing to do with it; the **rig** did. A **melody** was never on offer from an English **tower** or **steeple**.

An English tower bell hangs from a **headstock** on a full wheel: mouth **upward**, through a whole **circle**, resting mouth up on the far side and striking the **lip** each way. The lightest in a **peal**, the **treble**, may be 600 pounds; the **tenor** runs to a ton, its **tuning** and **crown** set at the **foundry**.

A ringer holds the **sallie** and can **hurry** the swing or check it on the **slider**. He cannot stop it, play it fast, or **muffle** it mid-**swing**. So no bell shifts more than one place at each change, the **clapper** falling at **backstroke** as at hand: not taste but the limit of what a **rope** does to a turning ton of **metal**.

The Low Countries hung their bells dead in the **belfry**, bolted at the **crown** above the **transept**, struck by **hammers** from a **keyboard** of wooden **batons**. A **carillon** plays any tune written for it; its player sits above the **crypt**, the **vestry**, a **tomb** and a font.

Given a **rig** that shuffles its notes one place at a time, the English wrote the only music such **rigging** makes. On ten bells the full set takes four months by a **rota** of **sexton** and **bellman**; only a **muffled** ring spares the **churchyard** and its **pews**. What looks like a national **disposition** is often the shape of the metalwork under the **yew**.
