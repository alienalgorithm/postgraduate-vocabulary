For 157 years Cambridge published the results of its mathematical examination in order of merit, and
the man at the top was the Senior Wrangler. Maxwell, Kelvin and Cayley all sat it. The place was
regarded as the highest intellectual attainment in Britain, and years were remembered by who had won it.

Hardy, who sat the paper in 1898, said afterwards that when the examination stood at the zenith of its
difficulty and fame, English mathematics was near its lowest ebb.

The training was not done by the university. Candidates hired private coaches who drilled them on past
papers and on bookwork, the verbatim reproduction of proofs under timed conditions. Attendance at
lectures fell away. Babbage, holding the Lucasian chair from 1828 to 1839, delivered none at all in
eleven years.

The syllabus hardened around what could be marked quickly. Continental analysis was neglected because
it was hard to set as a problem with a single scoring answer. The best coaches could pick the ablest
students, which kept their records up, which let them pick again.

The order of merit was abolished in 1909. Hardy went further and argued that the only harmless
examination is one with a low pass mark and no classes at all. A ranking is not a measurement of the
subject. It becomes the subject.
