The dead hand is the usual figure for the English **entail**: a **landowner** reaching out of the grave to **shackle** his line. The law never allowed it. An **entail** ran for lives in being plus twenty-one years and no further — a father, his eldest son, and that son's unborn son until his **majority**. Three generations, then it lapsed.

So the great estates were never **tethered**. They were **remade**, once a generation, by **covenant**.

The two sides held equal cards. The sitting man was tenant for life: he drew every **rent** and could not sell a field. His **heir** held the **freehold** in tail — owner of the whole thing in the future, and of nothing at all now. Neither could act without the other.

On the son's twenty-first birthday they sat down. He **ceded** his tail, taking in its place an income payable from that afternoon. The father **waived** nothing he actually had, and bought another generation of **resettlement**. Provision for the widow, **portions** for the daughters, allowances for the younger sons — every claim on the land was priced in the same hour.

The **perpetuity** was never in the **deed**. It sat in a **bargain** **refreshed** every twenty-one years by two men who each held one thing the other wanted. A dead hand **grips** nothing. What held these estates was a living hand, **bartered** with, and **remade**.
