The board had four corners, nine spaces to a side, a jail, a public park and a row of railway squares.
Lizzie Magie patented it in 1904. She also drafted two rulebooks for the same board.

Under the Prosperity rules, rent paid into a common pot and every player's counter grew richer when any
one of them bought well. Under the Monopolist rules you bankrupted your rivals and took their deeds.
Same squares, same dice, same tokens. The pair was the lesson: play both, feel the difference, draw the
conclusion yourself.

Almost nobody played Prosperity. The harsh rulebook travelled on its own for thirty years, copied by
hand on oilcloth, its streets renamed town by town, until an Atlantic City version reached an unemployed
salesman in Germantown.

Parker Brothers bought his version, then bought Magie's patent for five hundred dollars with no
royalties attached. He retired a millionaire at forty-six.

The company is the usual villain here, but the choosing had happened three decades before it arrived. A
demonstration with two halves keeps the half that is pleasant to win. Magie had built the argument into
the board and then left it to the players, who threw away the half that carried it.
