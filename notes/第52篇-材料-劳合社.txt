A surety works because the man standing behind the debt is outside it. He can vouch for the debtor
precisely because his own purse is untouched by the default. Reinsurance is that idea at scale: a
syndicate which has underwritten more than it can bear cedes part of the exposure to a separate pool of
collateral.

At Lloyd's in the 1980s the pools stopped being separate. Syndicate A reinsured its excess with B, B
with C, and C, eventually, with A. The excess-of-loss spiral was a closed circuit. One claim travelled
round it, each underwriter retaining a slice of premium and passing the balance on, until the same pound
of loss had been redeemed several times over from the same capital. Nobody built the circuit on purpose.
A court found later that even the men writing this business did not grasp what the spiral did.

Then the claims arrived. Asbestos policies drafted in the 1930s matured in the 1980s. Under the
year-of-account rules the member who forfeited his house was whoever held the syndicate now, not
whoever had signed the risk then. Membership peaked at 33,500 in 1988, the first of five loss-making
years. Sixteen billion pounds.

A guarantee is worth something only while the guarantor stands outside the danger. When everyone
underwrites everyone, a market has not spread its risk. It has lost track of where the risk sits.
