Everyone knows the insult: Shakespeare left his wife "my second-best bed." Three centuries of
biographers read it as a snub from the grave, a widow put in her place with the spare furniture.

A bed was the most valuable thing most households owned. A canopied bed in Shakespeare's day cost
about five pounds, half a schoolmaster's year; the featherbed alone took fifty pounds of feathers,
which meant years of plucking, and servant girls were allowed to keep the feathers from the poultry
they dressed and saved them toward a marriage. Emigrants to America packed the featherbed and left
the chairs.

So the best bed was not slept in. It stood downstairs in the parlour, hung with tapestries and a
tester, where callers could see it and, through the window, the street; it was for the important
guest, who seldom came. The second-best bed was the one in the chamber where the couple slept.

Wills of the time rank beds the way they rank land. One man leaves "the best featherbed" to a son,
"the second" to his wife, "the third" to another son, "the fourth" to a daughter; a yeoman leaves
his wife "my best bed and bedstead in the chamber over the kitchen." Nobody was insulted by
"second"; not even "worst" was an insult, since a worst bed was still a bed. The snub reading began
with a transcription error in a 1763 biography that dropped the word "furniture" and left the bed
standing alone.

The second-best bed was the marriage bed. What Shakespeare left his wife was the one thing in the
house that was already hers.
