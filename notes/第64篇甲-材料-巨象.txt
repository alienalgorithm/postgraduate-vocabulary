The story Barnum told was that on the night of 15 September 1885, at St Thomas, Ontario, Jumbo saw
the locomotive coming, scooped the dwarf elephant Tom Thumb into his trunk, flung him clear into the
ditch, and charged the engine head-on, knocking it off the rails and dying a hero.

The newspapers told another. After the show the two elephants were being led along the siding to
their boxcar when an unscheduled freight came round the curve. Tom Thumb was caught on the
cowcatcher and thrown down the embankment with a broken leg. Jumbo, walking behind, was penned
between the bank and the circus train with nowhere to go, and was struck from the rear. He died in
minutes with his keeper Matthew Scott beside him. The engine did leave the rails; not because an
elephant charged it.

Then the showman went to work on the corpse. The hide went to Ward's in Rochester, where Carl Akeley
stretched it on the frame to make the mount taller than the animal had stood — Barnum had billed
him at thirteen feet; he was ten feet seven. The skeleton went one way, the stuffed hide another,
each drawing its own crowd; the heart was sold to Cornell; the grease was bottled as ointment for
rheumatism. The stomach yielded English pennies, keys, rivets and a police whistle. The mount toured
with the circus for two years, then went to Tufts, where it burned in 1975; the ashes are kept in a
peanut-butter jar.

Alive he drew a crowd; dead he drew several. The last thing the showman stuffed was the story of how
he died.
