The Jockey Club states the **aim** plainly. A **handicap** exists to bring every **thoroughbred** across the line **abreast** — **alongside**, **simultaneously**, a **deadlock** of the whole **lineup**. Its own explanation adds that no **racecourse** has seen it. Six in ten British races are run this way.

Weight does the work. A **jockey** carries lead in the cloth to make the number, **colt** and **filly** alike. A horse's **rating** comes from recent form; better animals are **allotted** heavier **weights** to **equalise** the **contest**. Rous was **appointed** the first public handicapper in the 1850s, replacing rules that were local, **inconsistent** and never transparent. Today about six in ten British races are **handicaps**.

But consider the target. The **entrants** crossing together tell a **punter** nothing about which horse can **gallop** faster, and that is what a **spectator** at the **rail** came to learn. A perfect **weighting** does not **inform**; it goes **mute**, and the **impossible** case is the ideal one. Success would destroy the information the **spectacle** exists to produce.

The **ratings** move the wrong way for measurement. A winner is raised; a beaten **gelding** is eased. The **apportionment** answers the last race, never the **beast**. The number follows the last result, not the truth; and a **bunched** finish yields more **turnover** for the **bookmaker** than **runners** **strung** over a **furlong**.

Frankel reached an official mark of 140, the highest in the record. That figure survives because no weight brought that **sire** back to the others; he went off **favourite** at every **paddock**, the **outsider** beside him carrying stones less. A measure **chased** until it stops **discriminating** has stopped measuring. The **unattainable** **ideal** is the point: a **stalemate** reads as nothing at all.
