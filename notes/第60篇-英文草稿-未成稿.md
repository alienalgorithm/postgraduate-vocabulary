The **legend** is fixed. Every **biography**, **memorial** and **hymn** repeats it: the lady with the **lamp** reached the Scutari **barracks** in November 1854 and cut the death rate from 42 per cent to 2. It won her a **halo** no **reputation** could match, a **sainthood** the **pilgrim** still visits.

The rate rose after she came. The wards were **overcrowded** with the sick, not the wounded; **typhus** and **dysentery** killed, **gangrene** finished. In her first winter 4,077 men died; by February 1855 it was 42 per cent, the highest in the theatre. She had a **matron**'s eye for **lint**, **dressings** and a clean **cot**, named **overcrowding** to the minister, and blamed the food, not the **squalor** below.

The building sat on a broken **sewer**. A Sanitary Commission arrived in March 1855, six months after her, and did what a **lantern** could not: flushed the drains, cut **ventilation** into the wards, emptied **cesspool** and **latrine**, pulled a dead horse from the **fetid** water. **Filth**, **vermin**, **rat** went with it. Within months, 2.2 per cent. She never claimed the fall.

After the war she went through the returns with a **statistician**. Her own table showed what the **bedside** could not: the **garrison** hospitals at the front killed fewer. Cases kept at **Balaclava**, where **frostbite** and **scurvy** ran, and never put aboard a **hospital-ship** or **transport-ship**, died at 27 per thousand a month; **evacuated** to Scutari that February, 427.

She published it. That **dossier** against her own hospital made the case for public health law in **Parliament** and India. **Devotion** wrote the **sermon** to the **saint**; nobody read the table. She is an **icon** for the **contagion** she did not stop, and forgotten for proving that she had not.
