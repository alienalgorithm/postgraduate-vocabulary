The story is that George Hodgson devised a **brew** **potent** enough to reach India. Nothing needed devising; the **brewing** was old. **Ale** and dark **beer** had reached Calcutta since the early 1700s, arriving **bitter** and drinkable; a **cask** of the dark sort could be excellent after a year at sea, **lees** down, **dregs** clean.

Read the **auction** notices. From the 1780s the Calcutta **gazette** ran an **advertisement** for a **captain**'s cargo: pale ale, **stout**, cider — and small beer, the **weakest** **wort** a **tun** will **mash**. If small beer survived the passage, the passage did not demand **strength** or **hops**.

What Hodgson shipped already existed. October beer was **brewed** at harvest for the **gentry**, laid down two years in **butt**, heavy with hops, to answer the like purpose of wine. A manual of 1768 gives the **mash** and the **infusion**: 6 and a half pounds to 8 measures of **malt**, for a year's **durability**. That was written before Hodgson shipped a cask east.

His advantage was a **wharf** and a **slate**. The Bow works stood beside the East India **quay**; he offered **captains** an 18-month **loan**. His **heirs** proved **ruthless**: hearing of a rival **keg**, they would **undercut** until the challenger withdrew — which sent the trade to **Burton**.

The voyage was not the obstacle; it was the **ripening**. Four months of pitching and heat **ripened** the **casks** faster than a cellar, the **yeast** and the **lees** working throughout: at the **tapping**, what came off the **stave** was not what went in at the **bung**. The trade did not find a **liquor** that could survive the journey. The journey was the last part of the brewing.
