Every German beer label says it: brewed to the Reinheitsgebot of 1516, the purity law, the world's
oldest food regulation — nothing but barley, hops and water, five centuries of the same clean rule.

The decree of 23 April 1516, signed at Ingolstadt by the dukes of Bavaria, is mostly a price list.
From Michaelmas to St George's Day a Mass of beer was not to be sold for more than one Munich
Pfennig; from St George's to Michaelmas, two; the innkeeper's profit was capped; beer that broke
the rule was confiscated. Then the one sentence everyone remembers: it shall be brewed from barley,
hops and water and nothing else. The word purity is not in it. Neither is yeast.

The sentence was about bread. Wheat and rye made loaves; barley made poor bread and good beer, so
brewers were kept off the bakers' grain in a duchy where a bad harvest meant riot. It also shut out
the northern brewers' herbs, the gruit of bog-myrtle and yarrow, and the cheaper tricks — soot,
nettle, henbane — that stretched or stunned a cask. And it did not bind the men who signed it: the
Wittelsbachs kept a monopoly on wheat beer for their own table and licensed a single brewhouse to
make it.

The name came four hundred years later. A Bavarian parliamentarian coined Reinheitsgebot in 1918,
arguing to keep the rule in the new republic. In 1987 the European Court of Justice struck the
rule down as a barrier to trade, after French brewers complained they could not sell in Germany;
the labels kept the word anyway.

A grain ration and a price cap became a purity, then a heritage, then a brand. The rule did not
change in five hundred years. What it was for changed three times.
