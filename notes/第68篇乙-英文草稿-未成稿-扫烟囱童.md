In February 1875 a boy named George Brewster was sent up the **flue** of Fulbourn **asylum** near Cambridge, **scrambled** into a bend, **choked** on **soot** and **smothered**; a wall was pulled down to get him out. The **coroner**'s jury found his master guilty of **manslaughter**. Lord Shaftesbury took the case to the Lords, and the Chimney Sweepers Act of that year is remembered as the law that ended the climbing boys.

It did not ban them. The ban was thirty-five years old. The Act of 1840 had made it an offence to send anyone under twenty-one up a flue, and the Act of 1834 had fixed the shape of new chimneys — fourteen inches by nine, no sharp angles — so a machine could do the work. The machine existed: George Smart's **brush** of 1803, a **bristle** head on jointed **canes** **shoved** up from the grate, backed by a Society for **Superseding** the Necessity of Climbing Boys. For seventy years the law, the tool and the lobby were in place, and the boys went on climbing.

They climbed because a brush cost money and a boy cost less; because old flues twisted where a **cane** would not go and a **scraper** would; because a **master-sweep** bought an **apprentice** from the parish for a few pounds; and because nobody was paid to look. Shaftesbury told the Lords that London, with four million people, had no **climbing-boy** left; the deaths were in the provinces, where no one **enforced** what the capital had stopped doing.

The 1875 Act added two things: a sweep needed a **licence** from the police, a **certificate** renewed each year, and a policeman could take it away. That was all. Within a decade the boys were gone from the **cowl** and the **hearth**.

The law that is remembered is not the one that made the thing illegal. It is the one that hired someone to check.
