Picture a Dickens street and you hear it: iron-shod hooves and iron-tyred wheels clattering over
cobbles, the old stones of an old city, medieval underfoot.

The stones were not old, and they were not cobbles. A cobble is a rounded beach stone; what
London's main streets carried from the 1820s were setts, blocks of Aberdeen granite quarried to a
size, about eleven inches by thirteen, laid in courses across the road. They were an engineering
answer to one question: how does a horse keep its feet. Setts were cut with edges for a hoof to
catch, laid crossways so the edges faced the traffic, and after 1844 made smaller so there were
more of them. Very hard granite polished under wear until it was as slick as ice and had to be
replaced — Newgate Street's Welsh setts came up after two years — so the engineers chose stone that
wore rough.

The price of a hoof-hold was noise. By the 1840s the Times ran letters weekly about the din; Dickens
said the street outside his friend Leech's study had killed him. So the city tried the quiet
surfaces: macadam in the 1820s, which wore into holes under cargo carts; pine blocks outside the
Old Bailey in 1839, quiet and soft on the hoof, slippery in the wet, rotten in three years;
compressed asphalt in Threadneedle Street in 1869, smooth as marble, silent — and lethal to a horse
on a damp morning. Horse owners petitioned against asphalt; the RSPCA joined them; the New York Sun's
correspondent noted that the horses could not vote, or the asphalt would have gone at once.

Granite held the busy streets into the twentieth century, and wood was still on a fifth of London's
roads in 1929. The surface changed only when the hoof left it.

The cobbled street is not a relic. It is the shape of a compromise between the ear and the hoof,
and the hoof won.
