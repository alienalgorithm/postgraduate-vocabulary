The usual account has English **potters** unable to buy china **clay** and reaching for burnt bone as a **makeshift**. Thomas Frye **burnt** **cattle** **carcass** at **Bow** in 1748, ground the **ash** fine, and drew something white and **translucent** from the **kiln**.

The obstacle then went away. William Cookworthy found **kaolin** and **feldspar** in a **Cornish** **quarry** and took a monopoly on the hard **paste** in 1768. It passed to Champion, then to a Staffordshire consortium, and lapsed in 1796. From that year any **potter** could work the true **mineral** and sell the **wares**, **dish** or **bowl**, unhindered.

Almost none did. Josiah Spode had fixed his mixture around 1790 and it became the English rule, **moulded** and **thrown** in every Staffordshire kiln: six of ash, four of stone, three and a half of clay, **fired** twice. A **teapot** so made **chimed** when tapped; its **spout** and **lid** rang the same note, and the **ware** took **enamel** without a **hairline**.

Read the proportions again. The **kaolin** is in it. The **feldspar** is in it. The bone was never a **surrogate** for a missing **oxide**, because that mineral is not missing from the mixture. It is a third thing, added to both, and the **firing** takes the **biscuit** through a second **bake** before any **glaze** goes on.

What the bone bought was not supply. It was a **ware** that fired **whiter**, **chipped** less under a glaze, and held its light when thrown thin enough for a **saucer** to read a candle through. The **stopgap** outlived the want because it had stopped being an **expedient** long before anybody noticed — an **improvisation** that had become the **bloom** every **jug** was judged against.
