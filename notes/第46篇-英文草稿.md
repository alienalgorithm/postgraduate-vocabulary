On 20 June 1968 at Sacramento three **sprinters** were hand-timed at 9.9 seconds and all three were **ratified** as world records. The electronic figures for that same **sprint** were 10.03, 10.10 and 10.14. The **watches** had recorded a **tie** among men whose **chests** reached the **tape** eleven **hundredths** **apart**.

Hand timing was not **sloppy**; the men holding the **dials** were not **careless**. Three officials stood at the **finish** with **timers** and the middle value was taken — a **median**, chosen precisely to **discard** the odd one. What a **midpoint** cannot **reject** is an error the three of them **shared**.

A **timekeeper** **starts** on the **muzzle** smoke, which he must answer, and stops on an oncoming **torso** he has **watched** for ten seconds. His **reflex** runs late at one end, early at the other, so the two do not **negate**. They **add**. Every hand time is short by about a quarter of a second — an **unearned** **edge** for every sprinter — and the body fixed the **conversion** at 0.24.

Fully automatic timing became **compulsory** on 1 January 1977. The old **feats** were not **deleted** but **reclassified**, **relegated** to a list of their own. The ten-second **barrier**, **hailed** as broken that June, **stood** **unbeaten** until Jim Hines **ran** 9.95 in Mexico City that October — and that mark held for fourteen years.

The instrument had been **averaging** away the difference it existed to **capture**, **shaving** the same **sliver** off every man. A middle value covers one **scorer** who is wrong. It does nothing when all three **err** alike — that is not chance but a **skew**, and no amount, and and no amount of **repetition** will **undo** it.
