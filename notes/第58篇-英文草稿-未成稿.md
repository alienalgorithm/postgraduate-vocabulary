History likes a tidy cause. Before the collar, so the story runs, the ancient world **harnessed** horses by the **throat** and half-strangled them; after it, Europe's fields opened, the **granary** filled, the **serf** shed his **tithe**, and the **peasantry** got a surplus. One **buckle** of **leather** ends an age.

The claim rests on one trial. Lefebvre des Noëttes was a **cavalry** **colonel**, an **amateur** **antiquarian** writing his **memoir** in a **garrison**. In the 1920s he built the ancient harness from his reading of the pictures, cut the **thong** with an **awl** and **needle**, **hitched** a horse, and watched the strap climb till the animal could not breathe.

Look at what the pictures show. On **fresco** and **frieze**, on a painted **vase** or carved **stele**, the band sits low across the **shoulder**. An **excavated** **yoke** rode behind the **withers** and held it there. The horse pushed with its **breastplate**; nothing touched the **windpipe** at all.

Spruytte and Littauer rebuilt it in 1977 from **artefacts** a **museum** **curator** could hand them, not from drawings. Their **teams** pulled nearly as hard as animals in a modern **padded** collar — about 9.5 kilos of **pull** each with two riders aboard. Raepsaet's **replication** followed the same **protocol** and agreed; the **rebuttal** has held since, and no **consensus** now credits the strap.

So the medieval **collar** was a better one, not a first; **oxen** kept the heavy **tillage** for centuries. The **legacy** ran on by **citation** — a **footnote** copied into the next book until the **folklore** outlived its evidence. The colonel had tested his own handiwork and reported on antiquity. An experiment that builds its own **opponent** has settled the **upshot** before it starts.
