The **ordeal** looks like superstition at its worst. A man carries a red-hot **iron** nine paces **bare**-handed; the hand goes under **linen**; if it should **fester** when **unwrapped** he hangs. In 1215 the Church withdrew its **blessing** at the **altar** and a **jury** of sworn **jurors** took over.

Then read the **folio**. A **vellum** at Várad, 1208 to 1235, lists 208 **defendants** charged with **larceny** or worse. The court found a **felony** proved against 78. The other 130 walked away unmarked — nearly two in three **exonerated** by a bar of glowing metal, with no **bandage** wanted, no **surety** posted, no **pledge** taken.

Either those hands were miraculous or the metal was cool, and the **priest** held every lever. He **sprinkled** holy water over the **crucible**, lifted it from the **embers** with **tongs**, drew out the **liturgy** while it stood. The **congregation** watched from afar, past the **pulpit**. He alone **adjudged** whether the hand had **seared**.

Why bother? Because everyone believed. A guilty man made terms with his **accuser** or fled, and the **plaintiff** dropped the **suit**. Only one who thought himself innocent came to the **porch** and held out an open **palm**, **knuckles** up, and took the bar. **Willingness** was the evidence: fear of the **verdict** kept the guilty off the **porch**.

A **trial** no one faces unless innocent need not work; it need only be believed, the **scar** it threatens on the **flesh** doing the rest. The **sorting** happened out in the **chapel** yard by the **shrine**, before anyone reached the fire. An **oath** nobody breaks, a **wax** seal nobody cracks, a **deterrent** nobody calls: each works by never being used.
