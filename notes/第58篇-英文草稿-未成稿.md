The **ordeal** looks like superstition at its worst. A man accused of theft carries a red-hot **iron** nine paces; the hand goes under **linen**, and if it **festers** when **unwrapped** he hangs. Reason arrives in 1215, the Church withdraws its **blessing** at the **altar**, and a **jury** of **jurors** takes over. So the story goes.

Then read the **folio**. At Várad in Hungary a church **parchment** covering 1208 to 1235 lists 208 **defendants** who carried the **iron**. The court found against 78 of them. The other 130 walked away unmarked — nearly two in three **acquitted** by a bar of glowing metal, no **bandage** needed.

Either those hands were miraculous or the metal was not hot. And the **priest** held every lever of the **mass**. He **sprinkled** the holy water over the **cauldron**, which let him cool it. He lifted it off the **embers** with **tongs** at a moment of his choosing and drew out the **liturgy** while it stood. The **congregation** watched from the far end of the nave. It was he who **adjudged** whether the hand had **seared**.

Why bother? Because everyone believed. A guilty man expecting judgment settled with his **accuser** or fled; the **plaintiff** withdrew, the **defendant** never faced the **tongs**. Only a man who thought himself innocent would put out his palm. **Willingness** was the evidence, and a strong **incentive** kept the guilty away: the **ordeal** worked as a **deterrent** before it worked as a test. The rite merely made that **sorting** visible.

So a **trial** no one would face unless innocent need not work at all; it only needs to be believed. The **sorting** happens before the **ordeal** begins, among people weighing whether to submit, and the whole rite exists to make that choice dear enough to mean something. A **deterrent** nobody calls is doing its work by not being called.
