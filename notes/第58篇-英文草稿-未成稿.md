Every textbook says the ancient world **harnessed** its **chariot** horses by the **throat**. A **thong** across the **windpipe**, a **girth** round the belly, and any real load would close the **gullet**. The **collar** of the Middle Ages is credited with freeing the animal — **hoof**, **rump**, whole frame — and with it the ploughland of Europe.

The claim rests on one experiment. Lefebvre des Noëttes, a **cavalry** **colonel**, built his own **harness** in the 1920s, **hitched** a horse to it, and watched the thong ride up and shut the windpipe. He had cut the **leather** himself, **awl** and **buckle** and thong, from his own reading of the pictures.

Look at what the pictures actually show. On **mosaic** and **sculpture** the thong lies low across the **shoulder**, not the neck, and an **excavated** **yoke** sat behind the **withers** to hold it down. The horse pushed with its **breastplate**, the **tug** taken at the shoulder. Nothing crossed the windpipe at all.

Spruytte and Littauer rebuilt it in 1977 from excavated parts, not drawings: yoke where the finds put it, **strap** **buckled** low, cut with an awl as a **tanner** would. Their horses pulled nearly as much as horses in a modern **padded** collar, the tug taken clear of the windpipe; Raepsaet repeated it and got the same. Four could shift a load at 9.5 kilos of pull each.

So the medieval collar rescued nobody from **strangulation** at the gullet. It was a better one, better padded and **riveted** to shape by tanner and **wheelwright**, not a first one; **oxen** kept the heavy fieldwork for centuries after it. An experiment that builds its own **opponent** decides in advance how well the opponent does.
