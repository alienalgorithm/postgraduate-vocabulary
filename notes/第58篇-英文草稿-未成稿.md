The **ordeal** looks like superstition at its worst. A man clutches a red-hot **iron** nine paces **bare**-handed; the hand goes under **linen**, and if it **festers** when **unwrapped** he hangs. In 1215 the Church withdraws its **blessing** at the **altar**, and a **jury** of sworn **jurors** takes over.

Then read the **folio**. A **vellum** at Várad covering 1208 to 1235 lists 208 **defendants**, charged with **larceny** or worse, who carried the **iron**. The court found a **felony** proved against 78, on **oath**. The other 130 walked away unmarked — nearly two in three **exonerated** by a bar of glowing metal, no **bandage**, no **surety** paid.

Either those hands were miraculous or the metal was not hot, and the **priest** held every lever. He **sprinkled** holy water over the **crucible**; lifted it off the **embers** with **tongs** when he chose; drew out the **liturgy** while it stood. The **congregation** watched from the far end of the **nave**. He alone **adjudged** whether the hand had **seared**.

Why bother? Because everyone believed. A guilty man made terms with his **accuser** or fled; the **plaintiff** dropped the **suit**. Only a man who thought himself innocent put out a **bare** **palm**, **knuckles** up, to take the bar. **Willingness** was the evidence; the **incentive** kept the guilty away, and the rite made that **sorting** visible.

A **trial** no one would face unless innocent need not work; it need only be believed, and the **scar** it threatens on the **flesh** does the rest. The **sorting** happens before the **ordeal**, among those weighing whether to submit. An **oath** nobody breaks, a **pledge** nobody forfeits, a **deterrent** nobody calls, a **wax** seal nobody cracks: each works by never being used.
