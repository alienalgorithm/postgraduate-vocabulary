Dorothy Sayers put it down to national character: the Belgian plays a tune on his bells, the Englishman
works out permutations. The temperament had nothing to do with it. The wheel did.

An English tower bell hangs on a full wheel. It starts mouth upward, swings through a whole circle, and
comes to rest mouth upward again on the other side. The lightest in a ring may be six hundred pounds,
the heaviest a ton. A ringer holds a rope and can hurry that swing a little or check it a little. He
cannot stop it, and he cannot play it fast.

That is why no bell in change ringing moves more than one place forward or back at each change. It is
not a rule of taste. It is the limit of what a man on a rope can do to a ton of bronze already turning.

The Low Countries hung their bells dead, fixed in the frame, and struck them with hammers from a
keyboard. A carillon can play any tune written for it.

Given hardware that could only shuffle its notes by one place at a time, the English wrote the only
music such hardware can make: permutations. On ten bells the full set would take four months of
continuous ringing. What looks like a national taste is often the shape of the equipment.
