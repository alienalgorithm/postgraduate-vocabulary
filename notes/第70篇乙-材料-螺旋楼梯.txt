Every castle tour says it: the spiral stair turns clockwise so that a defender coming down has his
sword-arm free against the open well, while the attacker climbing up finds his right arm jammed
against the central post. It is on the guidebook, the panel and the audio tour at Arundel and at
Colchester.

No medieval builder wrote it. The earliest anyone has found it in print is 1902, in Spirals in
Nature and Art by Sir Theodore Andrea Cook — an art critic who was also a fencer, and who read a
stone stair the way a fencer reads a piste. From him it passed into a 1912 book on staircases,
into the standard castle histories of the 1930s and 1950s, into television, and onto the panels.

Then Neil Guy of the Castle Studies Group went and counted. About three in ten castle stairs turn
the other way. The Tower of London has anticlockwise stairs from the eleventh century, Newark from
the twelfth, Conwy from the thirteenth, Carisbrooke from the fourteenth, Kirby Muxloe from the
fifteenth — castles built in the middle of the Conquest and the Welsh wars, by men who knew what
a siege was. Caernarfon, Bodiam and the Tower have both directions in the same building. Nobody
built a stair for left-handed garrisons.

The likelier reason is dull: a right-handed man going down keeps his right hand on the wall. And a
stair is a terrible place to fight in either direction; the man above has a curved wall at his
back to catch his own blade.

The stair is medieval. The story about it is Edwardian, told by a man with a foil in his hand, and
it lasted because it is a better story than the truth, which is a hand on a wall.
